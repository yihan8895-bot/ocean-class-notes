Start with six questions, not six pages of equations

Chapter 7 explains how a small set of forces produces ocean motion. For a first reading, follow the causal chain below; then use three simple questions to test the idea before tackling the three concepts that are easiest to mix up.

7.1–7.2What pushes the water?

Acceleration and advection respond to pressure-gradient force, gravity, friction and Coriolis force.

7.3–7.4Why does the ocean form layers?

Temperature and salinity set density; buoyancy, stratification and mixing control vertical motion.

7.5How does wind move the upper ocean?

Wind stress, friction and rotation create Ekman layers, transport and pumping.

7.6Why can a current run along a pressure slope?

At large scales, pressure-gradient and Coriolis forces can balance to make geostrophic flow.

7.7How does a disturbance travel?

Vorticity and potential vorticity connect gravity, inertia-gravity, Kelvin and Rossby waves.

7.8–7.10How do these pieces build circulation?

Wind-driven gyres, equatorial and boundary currents, and overturning emerge from these balances.

3
Three easy entry questions

Try an answer first, then open the explanation.

01Does a water parcel travel all the way forward with a passing wave?

Usually no. The parcel mainly oscillates around an equilibrium position while the wave pattern and energy propagate. This is the simplest reason gravity, inertia-gravity and Rossby disturbances can all be called waves.

Remember: parcel motion ≠ wave-energy motion.

02How can I tell a surface wave from an internal wave at a glance?

Ask which boundary is displaced. A surface wave moves the air–sea free surface; an internal wave moves density surfaces inside a stably stratified ocean. Internal displacement may be tens of metres even when the sea-surface signal is weak.

Remember: visible surface versus hidden density interface.

03When may Coriolis force be ignored for a gravity wave?

Compare the motion with the rotation scale. Coriolis is secondary when |f|T ≪ 1, equivalently when the Rossby number Ro=U/(|f|L) ≫ 1. For a shallow-water wave this is also suggested by L ≪ Rd=c/|f|.

Short-period wind waves meet this condition. A tsunami crossing an ocean for many hours may not: rotation can influence its basin-scale path. Near the equator f is small, but its north–south gradient β still controls equatorial waves.

3
Three concepts that are easy to blur

Each confusion comes from mixing two different classification rules.

CLASSIFICATION

Wave names are not one set of exclusive boxes

Surface/internal says where the vertical structure is. Gravity/Rossby points to the restoring mechanism. Kelvin identifies a boundary-trapped dynamical branch. A Kelvin wave can therefore have a surface (barotropic) or internal (baroclinic) mode.

Memory cue: location, mechanism and geometry are three separate labels.
PROPAGATION

Phase speed and group speed answer different questions

Phase velocity follows a crest; group velocity describes energy and information. They may differ in magnitude or direction. Rossby-wave phase generally travels westward relative to the mean flow, while its energy can propagate differently.

Memory cue: watch a crest for phase; watch a wave packet for energy.
ROTATION & WAVEGUIDE

f=0 at the equator does not make rotation irrelevant

Across the equator, f changes sign and β=df/dy remains finite. This variation plus gravity and stratification traps equatorial modes in a waveguide. Equatorial Kelvin waves propagate eastward; equatorial Rossby modes propagate westward and have off-equatorial structure.

Memory cue: at the equator, replace “large f” with “changing f”.

LEVEL 1 · BRIEF ANSWER

The answer in one minute

Surface gravity waves disturb the air–sea interface. Gravity acting on the displaced free surface is the main restoring force, and the wave energy is concentrated near the surface. Internal waves disturb density surfaces inside a stably stratified ocean. Buoyancy, caused by gravity acting on density differences, is their restoring force.

Surface waves are generally much faster and have visible surface elevations from centimetres to metres. Internal waves are slower because density contrasts within seawater are small, but their vertical displacements can reach tens of metres or more while leaving only a weak surface signal. In a stable water column, a displaced parcel becomes heavier or lighter than its surroundings and is pushed back toward its equilibrium depth; overshoot produces an oscillation. A sharp two-layer density jump supports an interfacial wave, whereas continuous stratification supports depth-filling internal-wave beams and discrete vertical modes.

CHAPTER 7.7 EXTENSION · WAVE FAMILY

Put four ocean-wave families on one map

The table links the surface and internal gravity waves above to Kelvin and Rossby waves in the textbook. Read it from gravity and stratification, through rotation and deformation radius, to boundaries and potential-vorticity gradients.

ζ
Relative vorticity

Local spin of the horizontal flow: ζ=∂v/∂x−∂u/∂y. Planetary vorticity is f=2Ωsinφ.

q
Potential vorticity

For one shallow-water layer, q=(f+ζ)/h. With no friction or diabatic forcing, Dq/Dt=0.

Rd
Rossby deformation radius

Rd=c/|f| compares gravity-wave adjustment with rotation. The speed c may be an external or baroclinic-mode speed.

ω(k)
Dispersion

A dispersion relation links frequency and wavenumber. Its slope gives group velocity, so phase and energy need not move together.

σ
Geostrophic instability

Barotropic or baroclinic instability extracts energy from a mean current and can generate eddies and Rossby-like disturbances. It is an energy source, not another wave family.

LEVEL 2 · RESTORING PHYSICS

Why stable stratification permits internal waves

N² = −(g/ρ₀) dρ/dz > 0

Here z is positive upward. Stable stratification means density increases downward, so dρ/dz<0 and N²>0. N is the buoyancy or Brunt–Väisälä frequency.

PARCEL DISPLACEMENT

Move a parcel upward without immediately changing its density. It is denser than its new surroundings and sinks. Move it downward and it is lighter than its surroundings and rises. Momentum carries it past equilibrium, creating an oscillation.

STABILITY TEST

N²>0: stable and oscillatory; internal waves can propagate. N²≈0: nearly homogeneous and weakly restoring. N²<0: gravitationally unstable; overturning convection grows instead of a small oscillation.

Amplitude means different things: compare surface elevation for surface waves with isopycnal displacement for internal waves. Large internal displacement does not imply a similarly large sea-surface signal.
LEVEL 3 · INTERNAL-WAVE STRUCTURE

Interfacial waves versus continuous stratification

A

Interfacial internal wave

An idealized ocean contains two nearly homogeneous layers separated by a thin density jump. The interface moves up and down while the layers move in opposite directions.

For a long wave, c≈[g′h₁h₂/(h₁+h₂)]1/2, where g′=gΔρ/ρ₀. It is a simple model for a sharp pycnocline and can develop into large nonlinear solitary waves.

B

Continuously stratified internal wave

Density varies smoothly with depth and is described by N(z). Motion extends through a finite depth rather than being confined to one interface.

The vertical structure forms modes with nodes and phase changes, or propagating beams. Each mode has its own speed and structure; the first baroclinic mode is usually fastest and has the largest vertical scale.

SUBMISSION-READY MODEL ANSWER

Integrated response

Ocean surface gravity waves are disturbances of the air–sea interface. Water parcels mainly move in orbital paths while the wave form and energy propagate. Gravity acting on the displaced free surface provides the restoring force. Their behavior depends on wavelength relative to water depth: deep-water waves are dispersive, with ω²=gk and phase speed cp=ω/k, whereas shallow-water waves travel approximately at c=(gH)1/2.

Internal waves are oscillations of density surfaces within a stably stratified ocean. They occur because a parcel displaced vertically from its equilibrium depth has a different density from the surrounding water. Buoyancy accelerates it back; its inertia causes overshoot and sustained oscillation. With z positive upward, stable stratification has N²=−(g/ρ₀)dρ/dz>0. In a rotating continuously stratified ocean, freely propagating internal waves usually satisfy f<ω<N.

The two wave classes differ in at least four ways. First, surface waves occur at the free surface, while internal waves occur within the water column. Second, surface waves are restored by gravity acting across the large air–water density contrast, while internal waves are restored by buoyancy associated with much smaller seawater density differences. Third, surface gravity waves are generally faster; for example, a 10 s deep-water wave has cp≈15.6 m s−1, whereas the example two-layer internal wave travels at about 0.85 m s−1. Fourth, surface elevation is commonly centimetres to several metres, whereas internal isopycnal displacement can be tens of metres or more even when its surface expression is small.

In the interfacial model, two nearly homogeneous layers are separated by a sharp density jump. The displacement is concentrated at that interface, reduced gravity g′=gΔρ/ρ₀ controls the restoring force, and the long-wave speed is c≈[g′h₁h₂/(h₁+h₂)]1/2. In a continuously stratified ocean, density and N vary smoothly with depth. Internal waves occupy a depth range and form vertical modes or beams; different baroclinic modes have different node patterns and propagation speeds. Thus the interfacial model is a useful simplified limit of a sharp pycnocline, while continuous stratification represents the broader vertical structure of the real ocean.
